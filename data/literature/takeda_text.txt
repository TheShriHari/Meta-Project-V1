1.
Introduction
In polycrystalline metals, yield strength increases with
grain reﬁnement in proportion to the inverse of square root
of grain size, so called Hall–Petch relationship. In the case
of polycrystalline iron, the following relationship has al-
ready been established for yield strength (sy) in the grain
size region down to 0.2 mm,1,2) where “d” denotes the grain
size in micrometer.
sy [MPa]100600d [mm]1/2................(1)
After that, many challenges for ultra grain reﬁnement to
1 mm have been performed in conventional low carbon steel
and it was conﬁrmed that most of data are on the line3)
drawn by the above equation. As far as discussing the grain
size dependency of conventional low carbon steel, it is con-
cluded that the Hall–Petch coefﬁcient (ky) should be the
value of around 600 MPa·mm1/2. On the other hand, it 
has recently reported that the ky is very small (about
100 MPa·mm1/2) in IF-steel.4,5) This suggests a certain im-
purity dependency of ky in polycrystalline iron.
In this study, the carbon and nitrogen contents depend-
ence of Hall–Petch coefﬁcient of ferritic iron was investi-
gated by using various irons containing different contents
of carbon and nitrogen up to 60 ppm C or N. The mecha-
nism of the change in Hall–Petch coefﬁcient was then dis-
cussed in terms of the grain boundary segregation of car-
bon and nitrogen atoms.
2.
Experimental Procedure
The chemical compositions of materials used in this in-
vestigation are listed in Table 1. These materials are con-
ventional pure iron containing different amount of carbon
and nitrogen up to 60 ppm in total. The symbol of each
sample denotes the kind of element and its amount (ppm)
added intentionally, for example, C60 means the iron con-
taining about 60 ppm of carbon in total. In IF-steel, sufﬁ-
cient amount of Ti is added to ﬁx carbon and nitrogen as
Ti(C, N).

homogenized at 1 523 K for 3.6 ks and then hot-rolled to
10 mm thick. The hot-rolled plates were subjected to 90%

3.
Results and Discussion
3.1.
Microstructural Character of Specimens
Figure 1 displays the estimated amount of solute carbon
and nitrogen. The amount of carbide and nitride was ob-
tained by means of electrolytic extraction method, thus the
amount of solute carbon and nitrogen was estimated by
subtracting it from the total amount. However, from the dif-
ﬁculty of extraction of very tiny particles of carbide and ni-
tride, the amount of solute carbon and nitrogen seems to be
overestimated. The true amounts of solute carbon and nitro-
gen in IF-steel should be much smaller. In the following
chapter, the solute carbon/nitrogen contents were used for
data analysis when the effects of carbon/nitrogen on grain
reﬁnement strengthening were discussed.
The microstructure analysis by electron back scattering
pattern is one of the useful methods for grain size evalua-
tion because the informations on orientation distribution
and grain size distribution can be obtained at the same time.
For example, Fig. 2 displays the orientation imaging map
and the orientation distribution of crystal grains obtained in
the sample C60 annealed at 973 K for 90 s and IF-steel an-
nealed at 973 K for 120 s. The grain boundary in the imag-
ing maps represents the high angle boundary with misori-
entation angle more than 15°. These results indicate ran-
dom distribution of crystal grains. From the imaging maps,
the nominal grain size was evaluated at 27 mm and 30 mm
in the sample (a) and (c), respectively. The same mi-
crostructure analysis was performed for the other samples
and the nominal grain size was evaluated objectively in
each sample.

3.3.
Change in Hall–Petch Coefﬁcient by Grain
Boundary Segregation of Interstitial Alloy Ele-
ment
The change in ky due to interstitial alloy element has
been well known in nitrogen bearing austenitic steels.
Norström8) ﬁrstly reported that the ky of Fe–18%Cr–
14%Ni–3%Mo alloy was increased by the addition of nitro-
gen. Authors9) also systematically investigated the ky of
austenitic steels with different amounts of nitrogen, and re-
vealed the relation between ky and nitrogen concentration
up to 1.2 mass%. The contribution of nitrogen to ky has
often been discussed since the Norström’s report, and sev-
eral mechanisms have been proposed. They could be classi-
ﬁed to the following four ideas:
(1)
Nitrogen increases the twin density that appears as the
increased Hall–Petch coefﬁcient.8,10)
(2)
Planar slip of dislocations promoted by nitrogen en-
hances the grain reﬁnement strengthening.8,11–13)
(3)
Grain boundary segregation of nitrogen atoms stabi-
lizes the ledge structure of grain boundary working as
a dislocation source.12)
(4)
Dislocations are locked by a strong interaction be-
tween nitrogen atoms and dislocations.12)
However, each mechanism was only demonstrated indi-
rectly in a certain kind of steel, and there are also counter-
arguments for each mechanism. Although we have never
reached a consensus, the most frequently used idea, includ-
ing nonferrous materials, would be the (3) grain boundary
segregation theory. This is based on the yielding mecha-
nism proposed by Li14) that the grain boundary ledges are
supposed to be dislocation sources. As representative exam-
ples supporting this mechanism, a result in Ni–S alloy by
Floreen15) and that in Fe–P alloy by Spitzig16) were re-
ported. Both of the results concluded an increase in
Hall–Petch coefﬁcient with enhancing the grain boundary
segregation.

Here, on the assumption that the classical grain boundary
segregation theory would be applied to the case of this
study, namely, carbon in ferritic steel, the Hall–Petch coefﬁ-
cient was compared with the concentration of carbon at
grain boundary. According to the MacLean’s theory,17)
solute carbon concentration segregated at ferrite grain
boundary at 973 K is calculated by following equation:
[Cgb][at%C] exp(Q/RT)/(1[at%C] exp(Q/RT)) ...(3)
In this equation, [at%C], Q and R are the total carbon
concentration in solid solution, the activation energy of car-
bon for grain boundary segregation and the gas constant,
respectively. As for the value of Q, 78 kJ/mol estimated by
Grabke18) was taken.
Figure 6 shows relationship between ky and segregated
carbon content calculated by Eq. (3). ky value tends to in-
crease linearly with increasing segregated carbon content,
which suggests that grain boundary segregation of intersti-
tial alloy elements signiﬁcantly prevents the yielding of fer-
ritic steel. Only 60 ppm carbon causes marked grain bound-
ary segregation to 80 at%, resulting in the enlargement of
the ky from 100 (in IF-steel) to 550 MPa·mm1/2. The solu-
bility of carbon was reported to be approximately 60 ppm
even at ambient temperature19) in Fe–C binary alloy. There-
fore, it could be understood that the similar ky values,
around 600 MPa·mm1/2, have been reported in lots of previ-
ous studies on grain reﬁnement strengthening of ferritic
steels except for IF-steels. On the other hand, it is known
that nitrogen has less tendency to cause grain boundary
segregation in steel, as compared to carbon.20–22) This
agrees well with the experimental results that ky is hardly
inﬂuenced by nitrogen. The enlarged ky by nitrogen men-
tioned above might be due to the large amount of solute ni-
trogen in austenite matrix leading to the high concentration
of nitrogen at grain boundary even though the segregation
coefﬁcient is small.
5.
Conclusions
(1)
IF-steel has a low Hall–Petch coefﬁcient of approx-
imately 100 MPa·mm1/2. The Hall–Petch coefﬁcient of fer-
ritic iron monotonously increases with increasing solute
carbon content, and it levels off at 550–600 MPa·mm1/2
when the solute carbon content reaches 60 ppm. On the
other hand, the solute nitrogen hardly affects the Hall–Petch
coefﬁcient.
(2)
The increase in Hall–Petch coefﬁcient by solute
carbon seems to be derived from the grain boundary segre-
gation of carbon atoms. The small effect of solute nitrogen
on Hall–Petch coefﬁcient could be explained by the less
tendency of nitrogen atoms to cause grain boundary segre-
gation.
